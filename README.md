## What a cheap shared hosting plan actually gets you

For $1–$3 a month you're buying disk space on a server shared with other customers, a web-based control panel, and — on most current plans — free SSL, email accounts on your own domain, one-click installers for WordPress, and security patches handled by the provider. The host runs the machine. You upload your site through the panel and never touch a terminal. For most small sites, that's the entire job description.

The differences between cheap plans hide in the limits, because the marketing pages all look alike. One $3 plan might allow a single website on 5 GB of disk; another at the same price might allow unlimited sites on 20 GB. Storage, monthly transfer, site count, email account count, and whether SSL is even included: those five numbers do the real sorting, and they deserve more attention than the price tag itself.

This tier suits blogs, portfolios, brochure sites, landing pages, and low-traffic stores. If your project needs custom server software or root access, shared hosting is the wrong category at any price.

## How much storage and bandwidth do you actually need?

Less than the plan pages imply. A typical WordPress site with images fits comfortably in 5–10 GB; only media-heavy projects like podcasts, photo galleries, or download hubs need real storage. For a busy site, the monthly transfer allowance matters more than raw disk, because that's the number that runs out first when traffic climbs.

Map that onto the actual plans. RackNerd's 5 GB Bronze covers the overwhelming majority of first websites. ChemiCloud's 20 GB Starter is already generous for the category. If you're archiving video or running a download site, no cheap shared plan is the right answer regardless of the price on the label.

One anti-tip worth stating plainly: if you already run a site with real traffic, don't force it onto a $2 shared plan to save money. Downtime during a traffic spike costs more than the upgrade ever would. The signal to leave shared hosting lands around a few thousand visits a day, or the moment you need custom server software — at that point a VPS is the next step, not a bigger shared plan.

Open a support ticket before you buy, load a real site, click through the panel. If something feels off in week one, it gets worse in year two.

## FAQ

### Is $1-per-month hosting actually usable?

Yes, with expectations set correctly. IONOS lists paid entry plans at $1/mo and Natro at $0.89, and either can run a small site without drama. What you give up at that price is headroom: tight storage, tight transfer, usually a one-site limit. Free hosting is a different story — free tiers come with ads, throttling, and no support, which is why every serious comparison skips them.

### Can I run WordPress on a cheap shared plan?

Yes. One-click WordPress installers are standard across current plans in the $1–$7 range, and a typical WordPress site fits in 5–10 GB. Confirm two things before checkout: SSL is included (it is on most current plans), and email on your own domain comes with the plan if you want addresses like name@yoursite.com.

### How many websites can one cheap plan hold?

Entry plans usually allow one site; mid-tier plans open up to several or unlimited. On RackNerd's lineup, Bronze is capped at a single website while Silver and above allow unlimited sites, which is why Silver at $3.48/mo tends to be the better deal the moment a second project shows up.

### When should I move beyond shared hosting?

Around a few thousand visits per day, or when you need custom server software or root access. Until then, shared hosting is the cost-efficient choice; upgrading early mostly means paying for capacity you're not using.
